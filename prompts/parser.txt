Convert a problem statement into the typed structural representation
R = (E, Rel, T, A, G, F) that the search operates on.

Extract E, Rel, T, A, G, and F from P. Preserve explicit constraints, list
inferred assumptions separately, and do not solve the problem.

<problem>
{{PROBLEM}}
</problem>

Return one JSON object and nothing else: no prose before or after it, no code
fence, no commentary on your choices, no solution, no partial working.

Shape:

{
  "entities":    [{"id": str, "type": <entity type>, "sort": str, "label": str}],
  "relations":   [{"src": str, "dst": str, "rtype": <relation type>}],
  "assumptions": [{"text": str, "load_bearing": bool, "negated": false}],
  "goal":        {"objective": str, "constraint": str},
  "frame":       <frame>
}

<entity type> is exactly one of, and nothing else:

  constant   variable   parameter   input   output   coordinate

<relation type> is exactly one of, and nothing else:

  has   relates   depends_on   constrains   part_of   maps_to   equals
  bounds   indexes   applies_to   derived_from

<frame> is exactly one of, and nothing else:

  algebra   algorithm-design   analysis   category-theory   combinatorics
  dynamical-systems   euclidean-geometry   geometry   hyperbolic-geometry
  information-theory   linguistics   logic   number-theory   optimization
  physics   probability   projective-geometry   statistics
  streaming-algorithms   thermodynamics   unspecified

Fields.

  entities     1 to 50 of them. "id" is unique across the list, 1 to 64
               characters drawn from [A-Za-z0-9_.:-], and should read as the
               symbol or short name the problem itself uses (n, grid,
               target_sum). "type" is the entity's role in the closed
               vocabulary above; the collection of these values is the type
               map T, which is why T is not a separate field. "sort" is the
               free-form domain sort (side_length, memory_bound, residue) and
               may be "" when the problem supplies none. "label" is a short
               gloss, or "".

  relations    0 to 200 of them. "src" and "dst" must each be the "id" of an
               entity you listed above; a relation naming anything else is
               rejected. Direction is meaningful: constrains(a, b) reads "a
               constrains b", part_of(a, b) reads "a is part of b".

  assumptions  1 to 32 of them, each one statement, written in the
               affirmative. "negated" is false in every parser output.

  goal         "objective" is what the problem asks for, in one sentence.
               "constraint" is the condition the objective is stated under,
               in the problem's own words, or "" if there is none.

  frame        the discipline the problem is posed in. Use "unspecified"
               rather than guessing when no frame fits.

Explicit constraints versus inferred assumptions. These are different things
and the difference is the point of this stage.

  An explicit constraint is written down in P. Preserve it: the condition the
  objective is stated under goes in "goal.constraint" using the problem's own
  words, and the rest become `constrains` or `bounds` relations between the
  entities they hold over. Never drop, weaken, round or paraphrase away an
  explicit constraint -- downstream stages check the candidate answer against
  the original statement, so a constraint lost here is a wrong answer later.

  An inferred assumption is not written in P but is needed for P to be well
  posed: the ambient space, finiteness of a set, independence of samples,
  that a quantity is real rather than complex. List these, and only these, in
  "assumptions".

  Mark "load_bearing": true on each assumption the problem stops making sense
  without. At least one assumption must be marked true; if the problem
  genuinely rests on nothing implicit, say so as an assumption ("the
  statement is exact as given") and mark that one.

Do not solve the problem. Do not compute or guess the answer, do not sketch a
method, and do not invent an entity, relation or assumption that the problem
does not support in order to make it easier to solve.
